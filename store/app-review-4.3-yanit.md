## Gönderilen metin

Hello,

We have not resubmitted the app. Here are our answers to all nine questions.

1. Sayio teaches Turkish speaking children from four to fourteen the thinking behind arithmetic instead of drilling them. It holds 28 shortcuts over four age bands, such as making ten, adding from the left and the trick behind the nines. Questions are generated rather than listed, so the same sheet never comes back, and any one can be turned into something the child can see and count. The times table is taught by working facts out rather than learning them by heart: a hundred squares are really fifty five facts, and once the easy families are taken out only twenty one hard ones remain. Six times seven becomes five times seven and one more seven, and a spacing schedule decides what comes back and when. Nothing there is timed, on purpose, because a clock is where mathematics anxiety starts. Word problems follow Polya's four steps, and every shortcut is drawn on lined paper by a pencil the child watches.

2. Turkish speaking children from four to fourteen and their parents. Everything is read aloud in Turkish so a four to seven year old who cannot read the instructions can work alone, and at those ages answers are given without a keyboard.

3. There is no shortage of arithmetic apps. We built this one for what they leave out. They race the child against a clock, while we teach ways of working the answer out and keep the times table untimed, which is where the research we leaned on points: Baroody on derived facts, Gray and Tall on why children who memorise get stuck. They show a sum and not a method, while every shortcut here is drawn step by step with something concrete behind it. They are translated, so the voice and examples do not fit a Turkish classroom.

4. Two people test the app. One of us installs each build on his own iPhone through TestFlight, and his eight year old daughter uses the web build at ahmetercikan.github.io/Sayio. She is the age the app is for, so most changes come from watching her. The narration is now produced one sentence at a time, because she heard the end of numbers cut off when sentences were stitched from pieces. Every sentence is transcribed back and checked against what it should say, after she caught Turkish words being mispronounced. The lesson screen became two columns on a sideways phone after the number pad fell below the edge.

5. It stands on its own. Two other apps of ours are on this account: Cizio teaches drawing to children from five to nine and has been on Google Play through several releases, and Isim Sehir is a word game for families.

6. Cizio is a drawing school for five to nine year olds. Sayio is an arithmetic course for four to fourteen with its own progress record, reading behind it and parent section, and a child stays with it for years. Hidden inside a drawing app, no parent would find it.

7. There is shared code and we would rather set it out plainly. The three apps use the same stylesheet and interface shell, which is why they look related, plus small helpers for sound and for keeping a profile, and the same Capacitor setup. Sayio took that stylesheet from Cizio, our earlier app. What makes Sayio itself was written for it: the 28 lessons, the question generators, the drawings behind them, the notebook pencil and the times table course.

8. No. Apart from React, Vite and Capacitor there is nothing shared. No white label kit, no bought template, no generation service, no licensed content. We wrote the lessons and generators, drew the artwork and wrote the text; the narration came from a licensed text to speech service, from our own scripts.

9. Nobody asked us to build it and nobody supplied the idea, the content or the branding. It was written by Ahmet Mesut Ercikan, a developer on this team, and published under this account, which holds the copyright. No part of it came from a template or a generation service.

If a video or the research behind the method would help, we will send it.
